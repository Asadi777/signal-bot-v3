# Session 1 — BC Construction Electrician Exam Prep (CEC 2024)

Study notes for the student. Goal: pass the exam at 100%. These notes preserve the full detail of what the teacher taught and emphasized. Transcribed numbers that looked noisy are flagged "(approx — verify in book)."

---

## Exam strategy & logistics

**Exam structure — five blocks (A, B, C, D, E):**
- The exam is split into **5 blocks: Block A, B, C, D, and E.** Each block has a defined number of questions.
- **Block A = Essential Skills** ("Essential Skills"). This covers on-the-job practical things: how to use tools and equipment, how to write/follow safety procedures (e.g. scaffolding, a guard rail / support for a railing). The teacher says: *don't worry about this block.* It's not really "teachable" content — you learn it once, review it once, and "you climb the stairs and don't need to keep practicing it weekly." They cover it via Q&A. It has roughly **11 questions**, and students typically get about **8 of 11 correct** with light effort. (Numbers approx — verify.)
- **Block B = Wiring** (~30 questions implied). This is one of the two heaviest blocks. **"Part of it is inside the room."** Large number of questions.
- One block is about **~20 questions on "devices"** (the teacher names a block tied to devices/wiring — this and Wiring are the two big ones).
- **Block D = Motors.** This is the hardest section for most students. Comes with about **20 questions**. The teacher says **7–8 of the ~20 motor questions come straight from the book**; many of the questions that come from *outside* the book are actually the *easy* ones. He claims students who study with him score **above 80%** on motors (some above 85%), and he personally treats himself as an expert here ("I'm the god of motors," ~40+ years of experience, worked in generator manufacturing — Ansaldo Italy, ABB, Siemens). Out of 20 motor questions he says he knows what 19 will be about.
- **Signaling & Communication** = **10 questions.** Of these 10, about **half come from the book, half from outside the book.**

**The two heavy blocks (Wiring + the devices/installations block) determine pass/fail:**
- "If about **60 questions** come from these two blocks" and you do well on them, you pass — *even if you guess on the rest.* These two are described as the **"qualifying" blocks** ("تعیین قبولیه شما").
- He wants anyone to score **at least 90%** on these two big blocks. Scoring under 90% on something easy "is a black mark — it shows you didn't study evenly." These two are "very important for us."
- Counter-example he gives: a student wrecked Block A, B, D, and E (all under 70%) but scored **97% on Block C** — and that single high block "pulled him up" and he passed. So one block (and even one question) can flip your result.

**Pass mark and grading:**
- **Pass mark = 70%.** All questions are **multiple choice (تستی).**
- **70 itself is a pass.** He pushes hard on every point: students who got 69 had 56-something; the margin matters. *"One question can change your fate."* "I don't give up a single point."
- Recent cohort result he cites: **9 students sat the exam, 7 passed on the first attempt, 2 failed with 68.**
- He reviews failing transcripts like a **blood test** ("مثل آزمایش خون") — looking at the per-block scores tells him exactly where the weakness is (e.g. "you did well here, badly there"). He uses the block breakdown to diagnose.
- A near-miss example: a student got **68**; he could see from the breakdown that they under-performed on a service question they should have nailed, while scoring 85% on a part they'd never been explicitly taught.

**Time:**
- **You are guaranteed at least 5 hours.** A possible **6th hour exists but is discretionary** — sometimes the proctors give everyone 6 hours, sometimes only 2 of 10 people get it. Do not assume you'll get 6. **5 hours is always given.**
- Most students say **4 hours is too short**; some passed coming out in **3 hours 5 minutes.** **5 hours is more than enough.** Don't stress about time — but **keep an eye on the clock** under exam pressure.
- The teacher's own exam era was harder: no extra time, masks on, eyes red, very complex/heavy questions. He frames how much easier candidates have it now.

**His method / philosophy (how he teaches vs. other schools):**
- **He teaches mastery of the book first, then problem-solving** — NOT memorizing Q&A. Other schools drill question-and-answer, which (he argues) leaves you not fluent in the book. He wants you fluent in the *code book* so that on exam day you can locate anything fast.
- His claim: by the final days, "the book opens itself for you" — you know where everything is (e.g. you instantly know where high-voltage **bonding** is) without relying on a single marked spot. Even an answer he didn't mark, "the kids find easily" because they're fluent in the book.
- He teaches in **Persian for the concept**, then **solves in English** (since you answer in English). His priority: **understand the concept first**, then handle the English term.
- He contrasts with an Indian (Hindi-speaking) instructor at another school that students mentioned — hard to follow, half the words unclear; he learned the material there himself partly by not understanding half of it.
- He teaches **non-electrical students very deeply** (because they must learn definitions from scratch) and **also teaches Iranian electricians who have 20 years' experience**, because **"our definitions differ from theirs"** — Iranian-trained electricians have to unlearn habits. Analogy: teaching ping-pong to a tennis player is hard because of ingrained wrist habits; non-electrical students are actually *easier* to teach because they accept the new method without resistance.

**Question bank / updates:**
- His question bank is described as excellent and frequently updated. Students who just took the exam call him immediately afterward to report what came up, and he turns those into new questions. He claims **out of 100 questions, ~90 are new/current.** **If the practice questions he sent you later get updated, it means something changed** — treat an updated set as a signal.
- He stays "update" with the trade and the latest exams.

**What to bring / how the book is handled:**
- Exam is **open-book**; you use the **CEC code book** and your highlighting/marking.
- Questions come from the book, AND from sections he tells you to mark. Even spots he *didn't* mark, fluent students find easily.
- **He is removing the highlight/marking instructions on certain items** (see below): when something is **already a standard you've highlighted yellow**, don't add another mark. He's switching the student from "mark everything" to selective marking, because over-marking causes problems later (you can't find things).

**2021 vs 2024 code book (logistics of teaching):**
- The student has the **2024 book** (and also has the **2021** book). The teacher will **teach mostly from the 2021 book** because **~95% of the 2024 book is unchanged.**
- Changes in 2024 are mostly trivial number tweaks. Example given: a **transformer clearance to wall that was 6 mm became 6.5 mm — "big deal, not."** (Numbers approx — these are illustrative.) A few places have real structural change / a small formula change — he'll teach those (≈ **4 to 5 times in the whole course** they'll go to the 2024 book).
- **Problem with the 2024 PDF: it is locked.** Clicking Section 28 (or any section/table) does nothing — no hyperlink navigation, no jumping to tables/pages. In the **2021** PDF he can click straight into Section 10, jump to Table 43, show different pages, etc. So for teaching/navigation he prefers 2021.

## High-yield: what the teacher says is IMPORTANT / heavily tested

- **Bonding (definition) — RED-flagged.** The *definition itself* is asked **directly and frequently** ("جز سوالات پرتعداد"). Trap pattern: they give the bonding definition but **drop a word** ("low impedance path" → they remove "impedance," or break the sentence so it's incomplete). You must recognize the **complete correct sentence**: *"a low-impedance path that is permanently established to connect non-current-carrying metal parts."* He marks this RED (not yellow) specifically because it's tested directly. (Lines ~905–934, ~916–934)
- **AWG must be known cold.** AWG isn't usually asked as a definition, but you must master it because questions say "I have a #6 AWG cable, what's its max current?" — you need it for table lookups. (Lines ~924–926)
- **Ampacity** — "you'll deal with this a lot." Core to motor/cable selection questions. Know it as *the maximum current a conductor can carry continuously.*
- **Tables are 20% of the exam.** "**20% of the book's volume is tables; 20% of your exam questions are from tables.**" He says **be happy** about this because table questions are easy "lookup" plays — you just go to the right section, find the table, read the value. Master the tables.
- **Appendix B → ~6–7 questions.** Notes in Appendix B explain rules from the sections; questions are pulled from those notes. Standing rule: **if you go to a section and can't find the answer, check Appendix B.** ("This is the note of the day.")
- **Appendix D contains tables that "should have been" in the main tables — and questions come from there.** Specifically: **Table D1** (cables), **Table D2** (DC motors). He flags as a quirk/weakness of the book that 3-phase motors are in **Table 44**, single-phase motors in **Table 45**, but some motor data sits in Appendix D — and *that placement is a question source.* Weak students can't connect table numbers to their sections because the numbering isn't tied to sections (motors are taught in **Section 28**, but tables are 44/45 and Appendix D).
- **Index (back of book) saves you 4–5 questions.** Use the alphabetical Index for questions where you can't guess the section. Worked example: **"height of a fence"** — look up "fence" in the Index → it points you to **Section 26, Rule 304** → answer: a fence must be **no more than 1.8 m** tall. ("This part is very important for the exam — these 3–4 questions save you.") **Caveat: the Index isn't 100%.** Of 5 questions you might need it for, you'll find ~4; ~1 may not be findable. Example failure: **"chain-link fabric"** (the wire mesh / "chain-link fence") — he couldn't find it under "chain link"; it was filed **under the Fence topic**. Lesson: some terms are sub-entries of a parent topic. (Lines ~382–417)
- **Know the book's STRUCTURE.** Students who ignore this struggle. The six structural parts (preliminary/metric, sections, tables, diagrams, appendices, index) must be understood. "Very bad to read a book without knowing its structure."
- **Metric / unit conversion (front matter) is almost certainly tested.** The "Metric/Imperial" conversion column is the *only* part of the front matter that's a question source. Worked example below (foot-candle ↔ lux). (Lines ~76–123)
- **EMT is NOT a conduit — common mistake / trap.** Students keep asking "is EMT counted as conduit?" The book explicitly excludes EMT ("other than electrical metallic tubing") from the conduit definition. Know the distinction. (Lines ~1106–1180, ~1253–1256)
- **Feeder definition — 90% of electricians (even Red Seal holders) get it wrong.** Heavily emphasized; learn the exact boundary (between the service box and the branch circuit). (Lines ~938–986, ~1371–1386)
- **Branch circuit definition trips up ~90% of electricians.** Learn it precisely. (Lines ~935–996)
- **GFCI Class A** — "you'll deal with this a lot." Know where it's required (anywhere near water) and the trip threshold. (Lines ~1400–1533)
- **Emergency lighting → there is definitely a question.** Trap: it asks "which standard does emergency lighting / smoke detector get provisioned in?" The answer is **NOT always the NBC** — for *device sizing* (cable size, breaker) the answer is the **CEC**, while the *requirement to have it* is in the **NBC**. People who only do the AFCA/Red-Seal-style prep answer wrong here. (Lines ~1258–1367)
- **Object/Scope of the code book can itself be a question:** "What is the purpose of this code?" → safety standards. Mark/keep the book open to it. (Lines ~513–567)
- General: each **section has a different "weight"** — don't treat all equally. Some sections he covers in 10 per class; a heavy one may take a whole class. Easy ones (e.g. outdoor lighting standards) he passes over quickly.

## Content taught (in order, with full detail)

### 1. The code book's six structural parts

He says master the book's structure first. The book breaks into:

**(a) Preliminary / front matter (before Section 0).** Who wrote the code, its basis, sources, references — *not* exam-relevant **except one part**: the **Metric Units / conversion** section.
- They put a conversion column: **right side = "previously used units"** (imperial: inches, etc., what was used before) and the **SI units** (the metric system — millimeters, centimeters, kilograms, etc., the system Iranians already know).
- The whole code is written in **SI/metric** (mm, cm, km). In the trade, people speak imperial ("18 inches from grade," not mm). This is an *advantage* for SI-trained students: when the book says a receptacle height to floor must be **450 mm**, an imperial-trained person has to convert; the SI-trained person reads it directly. (450 mm ≈ 18 inches — he uses this to help the imperial-trained understand.)
- **Worked/expected question (lighting units):** illumination has a unit **lux** (SI) and **foot-candle** (imperial/English). If a question gives you lux and asks for the foot-candle equivalent (or vice versa), you **multiply by the conversion factor in that middle column.** This conversion column is "the only place in this first part" a question comes from. (Lines ~106–123)

**(b) Sections.** Numbered **Section 0 through Section 86**, but they are **NOT 86 sections** — there are about **43 actual sections** (numbering is by 2s / there are reserved gaps; he says "two/three sections are reserved at the top," so you actually read ~ that many). Each section is about one topic. Examples he gives:
- **Section 74 = Airports** (considerations for wiring an airport).
- **Section 66 (he says "66")** = environments that make film / motion-picture sets and similar (radio/TV, cable height where cars and people pass, etc.).
- Sections do **not** all carry equal weight. Some take a full class; some he rushes through.
- The CEC is a **hard book — it's not a textbook.** Even Canadian-raised or Filipino-raised students misread rules ("either I didn't understand, or I understood it wrong"). It's a mix of **law + technical/fne language** ("when something becomes law, it becomes mixed — law plus technical"). He simplifies it for you. (He compares it to Iran's engineering code — written deliberately hard.)

**(c) Tables.** "**20% of the book's volume.** **20% of exam questions are from tables.**" Table questions = lookups: they give you a characteristic, you find the number (e.g. a cable of a given size → what current it draws → find the value). Master the tables. (Lines ~202–225)

**(d) Diagrams.** **Not** technical/schematic diagrams as you might expect — they're more like **illustrations of physical appearance.** Example: **Diagram 1** shows the physical look of the receptacle types available in Canadian homes (he says there are 550+ types — number approx). They are NOT circuit/graphical diagrams. (Lines ~226–246)

**(e) Appendices.** **Appendix A through L, M, ...** (continues down).
- **Appendix B** is the one you work with. It contains **"Notes"** — explanations of rules that appear in the sections. **~6–7 questions come from Appendix B notes.** Rule of thumb: if you can't find the answer in the section, look in Appendix B. He recounts a fresh question last night he'd never seen — they checked Appendix B and the answer was there. ("This is the note of the day.")
- **Appendix D** is "general information" but contains **tables that arguably belong in the main table set** — and is a question source. **Table D1** relates to cables; **Table D2** is **DC motors.** (3-phase motors → Table 44; single-phase motors → Table 45.) He criticizes that the numbering isn't tied to sections (he'd have numbered motor tables 28-1, 28-2, 28-3 to match Section 28). But "if they'd organized it well, it would put us [tutors] out of business" — the book's difficulty is the reason tutoring exists.
- Appendices generally aren't all for Red-Seal candidates (some are for safety officers, manufacturers, engineers) — only Appendix B (and the Appendix D tables) matter for you.

**(f) Index (back of book).** Alphabetical list of technical terms. Critically helpful on the exam when you can't guess the section. (See the **fence → Section 26, Rule 304 → max 1.8 m** worked example, and the **chain-link fabric** caveat, both detailed under High-Yield above.) He notes this part is best illustrated in the **2024** book (his 2021 PDF lacks it conveniently). Practice with the Index until you can find things fast — but it's not 100%.

> He notes **Section 0 is not taught at most outside schools** (they assume a Red-Seal challenger with a 4-year apprenticeship doesn't need it). He teaches it anyway — especially to Iranian and non-electrical students — because the definitions differ from what you know. He has taken this class himself **4 times** (twice for Red Seal: failed 1st, passed 2nd; once for the EV-charger course; once for a safety/"FSR"-type cert) and has been deeply in the book through teaching.

### 2. Section 0 — Object / Scope

- **Object of the code = "development of safety standards."** Purpose: **"for installation and maintenance" of electrical equipment.** This can itself be a question ("What is the purpose of this code?" → safety standards).
- It covers **two domains: installation of electrical equipment AND maintenance/upkeep.**
- What outcome does it pursue? **"consideration has been given to the prevention of fire and shock hazard."** It **prevents** fire and shock hazard.
- Motivation cited: **~20% of fires in Canada are electrical** — the standard exists to reduce that (and to reduce costs). Keep the book open to this; mark it (but note: if it's already highlighted yellow as a standard, don't double-mark).
- After Object/Scope there's nothing else notable in the preliminary — go straight into **Definitions.**

### 3. Definitions (Section 0 terms)

Definitions are listed **alphabetically**, so related topics are scattered; he groups related ones together for you. *Learn some as a set, not one-by-one.*

**Ampacity** (line ~587)
- From **amp + capacity** → a current capacity. Specifically the **current-carrying capacity of conductors/wires.**
- There's an entity called a **conductor.** Ampacity of a conductor = **the maximum current you are permitted to pass through it continuously.** Example: if a conductor's ampacity is **15 A**, you may pass at most 15 A; above that it gets damaged.
- Exact-ish phrasing he gives: *"ampacity is the maximum current, in amperes, that a conductor can carry continuously."*
- Heavy use: e.g. "I want to install a motor — what ampacity must the chosen cable have?" (the cable must have capacity for the motor's current).

**Cable construction set — learn together: Conductor, Insulation, Armour, Jacket** (lines ~628–706)
- **Armour:** the steel wires/metal wrapping around a cable (in Iran called "armored cable" / زره‌دار). Purpose: **mechanical protection** — if someone strikes the cable with a pick/tool, it isn't damaged. Used in sensitive locations. Telecom cables (thin) are often armored; same idea in power. Tip: search **"armour cable"** / "armoured cable for electric" and use a **picture dictionary / images** to learn it visually. An example armored cable type he names: **"ACWU"** (transcribed "ای سین آی دی"/"ای سی دبلیو یو" — verify exact ACWU/AC90 designation in book).
- **Jacket:** the final outer covering. A **non-metallic covering** (contrast: armour is the metallic covering). Goes over the armour to protect it from moisture/acidic conditions (the metal armour would otherwise corrode). At a Canadian electrical supply store, ask for **"jacket"** — they may not understand "gaine/code" (French). Note distinction: **armour = metallic covering; jacket = non-metallic covering.**
- **Insulation (= عایق):** different from the jacket. Insulation does the job of a "shirt" on the conductor — it **prevents contact (short) between the conductor and the conductive parts.** The conductor is normally **aluminum or copper** (conductive); insulation keeps current from leaking to other conductive parts.
- So a cable = **4 parts: conductor + insulation + armour + jacket.**
- Naming: a cable **with armour** is called **"armoured"** (armoured = has armour); **with a jacket** = **"jacketed"**; **without a jacket** = **"unjacketed."** Some armoured cables have **no jacket.**
- Practical example: in industrial/commercial environments you run wiring **on the surface** (surface-mounted on the wall), so to protect it you either **run it in conduit/pipe** or use **armoured cable** — and you can use **unjacketed armoured cable.** (In a home you wouldn't do this.) In restaurants you'll see this everywhere around the perimeter — looks like a flexible shower hose; it unwinds if you twist it the opposite way. (Watch YouTube to learn how it's stripped — he tells students to YouTube the topics they cover, e.g. "armoured cable.") The cable type he points to here he calls **"ACWU"** (transcription approx).

**AWG — American Wire Gauge** (lines ~719–768) — important for cable sizing
- He calls the imperial-style sizing one of the **weaknesses of the Canadian standard.** In Iran you size by **cross-sectional area in mm²** (e.g. "1.5" = 1.5 mm² for lighting, "2.5" = 2.5 mm² for receptacles, "0.5" = 0.5 mm² for thin phone wire). Here you must use the table.
- **Table 2** (the cable table) has the relationship. **Two units of measure appear:**
  - **AWG (American Wire Gauge)** for smaller cables — up to about **107 mm²** (number approx — verify).
  - **kcmil (kc-mil / "kaysi mil")** for larger cables — from about **127 mm²** up to **2000** (i.e. cable size 2000). (Numbers approx — verify against Table 2.)
- **Do NOT confuse the two units** — their numeric ranges are very different.
- **AWG behavior: the smaller the number, the LARGER the cable (and the more current it carries).** Examples: **14** for lighting (low current), **12** for kitchen receptacles, **8** for a dryer/oven (high current). As the AWG number drops, ampacity rises (e.g. 15 A, 20 A, 30 A, 40 A...).
- The single-zero sizes are written as **1/0, 2/0, 3/0, 4/0** ("one-aught, two-aught, three-aught, four-aught"). When a spec says "**1/0 cable**" read it exactly that way (with the slash). These differ from sizes 1, 2, 3, 4 — i.e. 1/0, 2/0, 3/0, 4/0 are NOT the same as 1, 2, 3, 4.
- After 4/0 you go to **250** (kcmil), then larger; kcmil numbers do NOT map directly to the AWG sizes. kcmil also has an inch / cross-sectional-area relationship you can Google later.

> Aside: ESA/Red Seal recognition — he notes the Canadian standard differs from the US; if you want to work in the US (or some EU countries) check which jurisdictions recognize your Red Seal (search for the recognized list).

**Concealed vs Exposed (wiring terms)** (lines ~999–1035)
- **Concealed** = covered/hidden. Used for **in-wall wiring.** In your room you see no wiring; before drywall goes up, the electricians do the rough-in wiring, then drywall covers it. The wiring becomes **permanently inaccessible** by the structure once the building is finished. (Iran term: "تو کار" / in-the-work.)
- **Exposed** = wiring that is **accessible / on the surface** (visible, like in an exhibition/showroom — "exposed" implies on display/visible). (Iran term: "رو کار" / surface.)
- So wiring is either **concealed** or **exposed.**
- **Bare conductor** = a bare/uninsulated wire (سیم لخت) — a conductor with no insulation.

**Conduit** (lines ~1040–1185) — students often stumble here
- A **conduit** is a **raceway** with a **circular cross-section.** (And again, *raceway* is where students stumble.)
- **Raceway** = any channel/duct through which you run wire — could be a pipe, could have a round cross-section, could have a rectangular cross-section, metal or non-metal. **"Raceway" is a generic/common noun, not the name of one specific product** — you can't walk into a store and ask for "a raceway" (they'd look at you funny), just like ordering "food" instead of a specific dish.
- **Conduit = a raceway whose cross-section is round/circular** ("circular cross-section"), like a pipe. Types:
  - **Flexible metal conduit** — bendable by hand, can be shaped/curved easily.
  - **Rigid metal conduit** — stiff; you must bend it with a pipe/conduit bender.
  - **Rigid PVC conduit** — non-metallic (PVC), or other materials.
- That's the conduit concept; most electricians know this.

**EMT vs conduit — key trap** (lines ~1106–1185, ~1249–1256)
- **EMT = Electrical Metallic Tubing.** The code says: a raceway "**other than** electrical metallic tubing" — i.e. **EMT is explicitly NOT classified as a conduit.**
- Students keep asking "isn't EMT a conduit? it's metal, it's round, it's used in electrical." Answer: **No — EMT is not a conduit.** The book makes the distinction explicit.
- **Difference vs rigid metal conduit:** EMT is **thin-walled tubing** (low wall thickness); conduit is thicker-walled. (He'll show the shapes in class.)
- **Why use EMT then?** It's the **cheaper, easier-to-install, easier-to-carry option** — about **a third of the price.** This matters in estimating: he does project estimating; on a $200k project the margin can be ~$2k, and changing conduit vs EMT can swing project price by ~$10k. (Numbers illustrative.)
- **Where you must use conduit (not EMT):** mechanically exposed/at-risk runs — e.g. across a parking area where a car could hit it, or where it must withstand impact; there you use a piece of **conduit.** Along the protected length of the run you use **EMT.**
- He shares that students with 5–10 years' experience often never knew the difference, because on the job the supervisor just handed them a buy-list ("buy EMT") and they bought it without thinking about the distinction.
- Also mentioned in this family: **ENT = Electrical Non-metallic Tubing** — a raceway, round, but **NOT a conduit** (e.g. a flexible "trunk-like" tube). Term to know.

**Dwelling unit** (lines ~1203–1248) — two meanings; read it from context
- **General meaning:** residential locations overall — includes **house, townhouse, condo, hotel.** Book phrasing: *"one or more rooms..."* (for a person/persons). If a rule says "in a dwelling unit do X," it generally means residential.
- **"Other than dwelling unit"** = **non-residential** (can be commercial or industrial).
- **"Single dwelling unit"** = a **single/detached** dwelling → **house / townhouse.** (Whereas the broader "dwelling unit" can include apartments and similar.) So:
  - "other than dwelling unit" → non-residential.
  - "single dwelling unit" → detached units (house, townhouse).
  - plain "dwelling unit" → can include apartments / semi-detached.
- He notes these context distinctions aren't spelled out in one place in the book — they're learned from experience by comparing how the term is used in different rules.

**Bonding & Grounding** (lines ~784–934) — foundational; watch YouTube animations
- He strongly recommends watching YouTube (especially cartoon/animation videos) for **bonding and grounding** — easier to grasp visually.
- **Setup / why we ground:** Take a washing machine — metal body, electrical appliance. A wire inside can come loose and **energize the metal body.** If the body is energized and a person standing on the ground (or on tile, etc.) touches it, current wants to flow to ground **through the person** → shock. Other appliances pose the same risk: fridge, dryer, etc.
- **Two electrical laws he states (both important):**
  1. **Electricity always wants to return to ground / earth.** (Foundation of grounding & bonding.)
  2. **Electricity always takes the path of least resistance.** ("This is very important — the basis of grounding and bonding.")
- **Neutral / earth concept:** The generating station produces the **phase (hot)** wire; it does NOT produce the **neutral.** The phase is brought to your house; the current must return. The neutral's return is referenced to earth — a metal **electrode** is driven into the ground so the earth performs the "neutral" function (earth = the zero/reference point). Analogy: like potential energy of an apple held at height — it falls to the ground because earth attracts it; electricity likewise goes to ground. (Difference of potential.)
- **Grounding:** Run a **green wire** from the panel; in the panel there's a bus/terminal (the bus bar detail isn't critical), take that wire down to a rod/electrode driven into the earth. **The conductor + electrode + everything bonded to it = the grounding system** ("grounding" = connecting to earth). In Iran they call the whole thing "earthing"/"ارت" (= "earth" in English).
- **What grounding does in a fault:** If a metal body becomes energized, the green/ground wire gives the fault current a **low-resistance path back** — effectively it's like connecting phase to neutral, which makes the breaker **trip** ("تق می‌پره"). Without it, the fault current would flow through a person. Current chooses the **low-resistance ground path** (3 m into the earth) over the high-resistance path through a human body.
- **Bonding:** The portion that connects **from the panel out to all the equipment** — one wire to the washing machine, one to the fridge, one to the dryer, etc. **Connecting all these metal parts together = bonding** ("you've bonded them together"). In Iran both bonding and grounding are loosely called "earth," but the code separates them.
- **Bonding (exact definition — RED, tested directly):** *"a **low-impedance path** that is **permanently** established to connect **non-current-carrying metal parts**."* He notes "impedance" here just means resistance — he'd have written "low resistance path," but the code uses the technical "low impedance." What part of the washing machine do you bond? The **metal parts** (the non-current-carrying metal parts) — *not* the inner parts that are supposed to carry current. **Exam trap:** they give this sentence but **drop "impedance"** or otherwise break it so it's incomplete; you must identify the **complete** correct sentence and mark it. Even Red-Seal holders fumble the bonding/grounding definitions.

**Branch circuit, Service, Feeder** (lines ~935–997, ~1371–1386) — learn together; feeder is the trap
- **Service:** the supply wiring coming into the building — the cable that enters and feeds the **main panel.** "Service conductor," "service equipment," "service box" refer to this incoming portion. (e.g. "service conductor" = the wire entering the building.)
- **Branch circuit:** the wiring **after the final overcurrent device** going out to outlets/loads — the part you wire from the panel to a light, dryer, receptacle, fridge, etc. **Definition:** *"that portion of the wiring that lies between the **final overcurrent device** (its protection) and the **outlet(s)**."* The outlet can be a receptacle, a dryer connection, a fridge receptacle, lighting — anything. **"Final overcurrent device"** means there is **no further fuse/breaker after it.** ~90% of electricians fumble the branch-circuit definition.
- **Feeder (heavily emphasized — ~90% get it wrong, even Red Seal):** the conductors **between the service box and the branch-circuit overcurrent device.** Setup: a building owner has a **main panel**; two tenants each get a **sub-panel.** The sub-panels draw power from the main panel → you have one **main panel** and two **sub-panels.** The wire **in between** (main panel → sub-panel) is the **feeder.** Its boundary: **from the breaker in the service box to the breaker in the sub-panel.** Book phrasing: *"any portion of the electrical circuit **between the service box** (or equivalent) **and the branch-circuit** overcurrent device."* Examples of feeders: main panel → workshop panel in a factory; mall main panel → each store's panel (and onward = "sub-feeder"); apartment building main panel → each suite's panel.
  - Field heuristic he gives: the **thick/heavy cables** run through a building (e.g. the heavy cable to the electric range) — those are feeders, whereas the range's own final run is the branch. (He notes he wouldn't phrase it that loosely, but the book's plain-language editor — a non-electrician "editor" who finishes the sentences — wrote it simply.)
  - Quick check sequence on a 200 A house example: he could give a tenant a **60 A** breaker (a 60 A feeder) feeding the sub-panel.

**Emergency lighting** (lines ~1258–1367) — there will be a question; standard-source trap
- Definition has **two purposes:** so people can **exit safely** AND so the **facility/operation can be carried on** (it lights the way) during an emergency (fire, etc.).
- Concept: cinema exit signs above doors ("EXIT") stay lit on **battery** if the power/breaker trips, lighting the escape route through corridors. The whole arrangement = **emergency lighting**, part of **building safety.**
- **Standard-source trap:** building safety items (emergency lighting, smoke detectors) are **provisioned in the NBC — National Building Code** (abbrev. NBC). The **CEC** (Canadian Electrical Code) is what you're studying; it differs from the NBC. The NBC is also used by gas fitters, civil engineers, etc. — the requirement/placement of safety devices (e.g. smoke vs heat detector in a kitchen) lives in the NBC (or provincial/BC building code).
  - **BUT:** if a question asks about **sizing the emergency lighting** (its **cable size**, the **breaker/fuse**), that is **in the CEC**, not the NBC. People who only do basic prep answer "NBC" and get it wrong. **Read the question carefully:** "where is the device provisioned/required?" → NBC; "what cable size / breaker for it?" → CEC.
  - Real anecdote: a 12-storey government seniors' building renovation — the smoke-detector spec question went to a fire-safety officer / the NBC side, not the electrician; he flagged that it wasn't his scope (NBC, not CEC).
  - Another emergency-lighting angle: **exit-sign colors** (which colors are required — they're red) — "in which code are the exit-sign colors defined?" → it's **not in the CEC** (so it'd be the NBC). Be alert to whether the question is about the electrical sizing (CEC) or the safety requirement (NBC).

**GFCI — Class A** (lines ~1400–1533) — you'll use this a lot; know the trip value
- **GFCI = Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter** ("interrupter" = cuts off the circuit) — interrupts/cuts a ground-fault circuit toward the load. There are several GFCI types; the one in homes/buildings is **Class A** (there's no relevant "Class B" for this purpose — Class A is defined to distinguish it from older GFCIs).
- **Where required:** wherever there's **risk of shock involving water** — bathroom sinks, kitchen counters near sinks, **all outdoor receptacles**, near showers/tubs, and anything that could contact water.
- **Scenario:** a bathroom receptacle for a hair dryer/shaver. If a hair dryer falls into a water-filled sink, or a kitchen appliance falls in water, an ordinary receptacle would leave the water/sink energized and shock you when you reach in. A **Class A GFCI** interrupts and saves you.
- **How it works (with worked numbers):** A receptacle has a **hot** wire in and a **neutral** wire back. Rule: **the current going out on the hot must equal the current returning on the neutral.** If hot = 5 A and neutral = 5 A, fine. If hot = 5 A but neutral = 4 A → **1 A is leaking** somewhere (not following the intended path) — e.g. through the water/the metal sink to earth — i.e. when the appliance fell in the water, ~4 A returns via the neutral and ~1 A leaks through water/sink to ground. (He flags these numbers are illustrative, not exact.) The GFCI senses this **imbalance** and trips.
- **Trip threshold (exam value):** the GFCI must trip at about a small leakage; he cites it triggers at roughly **6 mA**, with the regulation stating it **must not exceed ~4–5 mA** for nuisance tolerance — i.e. the code sets it so it trips at **no more than ~5 mA** (the ~5 mA tolerance is built in so it doesn't nuisance-trip, but it must operate by ~4 mA region). **(Verify exact value: CEC Class A GFCI ≈ 5 mA trip.)** Small "faults" that aren't real are tolerated below this.
- **GFCI vs ordinary breaker:** an ordinary breaker has one outgoing line. A GFCI device has a **return path too** — it monitors the neutral return, detects the hot/neutral imbalance, and trips. Some homes have **GFCI receptacles** (with Reset/Test buttons on the face); GFCI protection can also be at the **breaker** in the panel (a GFCI breaker), so the receptacle itself may not show Reset/Test buttons even though it's GFCI-protected.
- **Where they're installed:** near every sink, near every shower/tub, all exterior receptacles — anything that could contact water.

**Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter (GFCI), Class A — definition wording** (lines ~1388–1422)
- He starts the definition: a device "**that will interrupt**" the circuit toward the **load** when a ground fault occurs. (He distinguishes there are also **pulse/other** GFCI types not used in homes.) The home/building one is **Class A.**
